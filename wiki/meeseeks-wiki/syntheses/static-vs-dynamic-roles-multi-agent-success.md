# Static Roles vs. Dynamic Roles: What Actually Predicts Multi-Agent Success

The hypothesis under test — that multi-agent LLM systems with a **static set of agents in explicitly, permanently defined roles** (a fixed team composition, unchanging for the whole run, as in MetaGPT's five-role pipeline or a CrewAI crew) are among the more successful patterns — turns out to bundle two separable claims that the evidence supports very differently. Fifteen sources gathered specifically to test it, read alongside what this wiki already knew from [AgentNet's decentralized specialization](../concepts/multi-agent-coordination-theory.md#concrete-coordination-mechanisms-without-central-control) and [MAST](../concepts/multi-agent-failure-modes.md), point to a clear verdict: **the hypothesis as stated should be rejected. The evidence that survives strongly supports a narrower, different claim: explicit role differentiation beats no differentiation, but permanent fixedness is not what's doing the work — and where the comparison is cleanly isolated, dynamic/adaptive role allocation tends to beat static allocation, not the reverse.**

## What's actually confirmed: specialization beats homogeneity

Every controlled comparison in this source set that varied *role differentiation* while holding other factors constant found the same result, and this is the most robust finding of the whole investigation. MetaGPT's own ablation (`metagpt-role-specialization-ablation.md`) shows executability climbing from 1.0 to 4.0 out of 4 and human-revision rounds dropping from 10 to 2.5 as roles are added one at a time to a bare pipeline, with role-free AutoGPT scoring only 1.0. AgentGroupChat-V2's MATH-100 ablation (`agentgroupchat-v2-specialized-vs-general-roles.md` and its companion capture `agentgroupchat-v2-specialized-vs-homogeneous-roles.md`) is the sharpest version of this: specialized-role accuracy rises from 32.5% to 53.5% as team size grows from 2 to 5 agents, while an otherwise-identical *homogeneous* team (every agent given the same "math expert" identity) actually degrades from 34.5% to 31.5% over the same scaling — opposite trends attributed to redundant, overlapping contributions when agents aren't differentiated. "Multi-Agent Teams Hold Experts Back" (`multi-agent-teams-hold-experts-back.md`) documents the failure mode from the other side: unconstrained, undifferentiated teams lose up to 41.1% against their own single best expert agent, because unstructured deliberation tends toward "integrative compromise" — averaging opinions rather than weighting genuine expertise. And a direct follow-up to [MAST](../concepts/multi-agent-failure-modes.md)'s "disobeying role specification" failure mode (`quantitative-role-clarity-multi-agent-collaboration.md`) found that a trainable role-clarity metric cuts role-overstepping from roughly 45% to under 10% and modestly improves task success — clarity and enforcement of *whatever* role an agent holds matters, independent of whether that role is fixed or reassigned. CrewAI's own design guidance (`crewai-crafting-effective-agents-role-guidance.md`) asserts the same thing anecdotally, while hedging that 80% of design effort should go to task definition rather than role definition — a tell that even a framework built around static roles doesn't treat staticness itself as the load-bearing variable.

None of this is evidence for *permanence*. AgentGroupChat-V2's own architecture assigns its specialized roles adaptively per task rather than fixing them for a whole run, and AgentVerse's recruitment mechanism (`agentverse-dynamic-expert-recruitment.md`) generates roles at runtime and still beats solo/chain-of-thought baselines. The variable that keeps moving the needle across this entire cluster of sources is **differentiation** — distinct, non-redundant responsibilities — not **fixedness**.

## What's rejected: static specifically beating dynamic

Where sources isolated *fixed-for-the-run* role assignment against *runtime-adaptive* allocation, holding team size, models, and task constant, the dynamic condition won, usually by a wide margin. The cleanest case is LATTE (`latte-adaptive-task-graphs.md`, with `latte-adaptive-task-graphs-language-agent-teams.md` as a complementary capture of the same paper): benchmarked head-to-head against MetaGPT's fixed five-role pipeline, a static Leader-Worker hierarchy, and — critically — against its *own* architecture with the single change of freezing the coordination graph after initial planning, LATTE's full adaptive version scored 79.7% task accuracy versus 57.6% for the static-graph ablation (a 22-point gap attributable to nothing but the ability to keep adapting) and 33.9% for MetaGPT, while using roughly a fifth of MetaGPT's tokens and less than a quarter of its wall-clock time. This is as close to a controlled single-variable test as this literature offers, and the isolated variable — dynamic vs. static allocation, everything else held constant — favors dynamic decisively.

DyLAN's dynamic team-membership selection (`dylan-dynamic-agent-team-optimization.md`) adds +13.0–13.3% on MATH/HumanEval and up to +25.0% on MMLU subjects over an unoptimized static baseline, with a smaller *optimized* three-agent team beating a larger four-agent *static*-debate team — evidence that adaptive team composition can substitute for raw team size. A 25,000-run, 8-model study (`self-organizing-agents-outperform-designed-structures.md` and `drop-the-hierarchy-and-roles-self-organizing-agents.md`, two independent captures of the same paper) found a hybrid fixed-order/autonomous-role protocol beating centralized/static coordination by 14% and pure unstructured autonomy by 44%. Meta-Team's longitudinal self-evolving teams (`meta-team-collaborative-self-evolution.md`) beat static hand-crafted multi-agent systems by 6.6% on average. And Solo Performance Prompting's persona ablation (`solo-performance-prompting-persona-ablation.md`) found dynamic, task-specific personas beating a fixed generic persona set by up to 41 points on Codenames — though this comparison is weaker evidence than the others, since the "fixed" condition there conflated fixedness with genericness rather than isolating fixedness against an equally well-specialized static alternative.

This is also where AgentNet's already-ingested finding fits: its fully decentralized, dynamically-evolving DAG routing scored 92.86% on MATH against MetaGPT's reported 53.00% ceiling on Logical QA under a comparable backbone — the same directional result LATTE later confirmed with a tighter experimental design, from an independent research group. Two architecturally different papers, run at different times, both benchmarking against MetaGPT by name, land on the same conclusion.

## The moderating factor that keeps the verdict from being absolute

The 25,000-run study is the one source that prevents "dynamic always wins" from being the clean takeaway: for a weaker model (GLM-5), self-organization *decreased* output quality by 9.6% relative to rigid, pre-designed structure. TeamBench's verifier-role finding (`teambench-verifier-role-overhead.md`) makes a related point from a different angle — a specific, well-defined *fixed* role (an LLM Verifier in a Planner/Executor/Verifier pipeline) was net-negative because the verifier falsely approved 49.4% of failing submissions, showing that a fixed role doesn't help just because it's fixed and well-specified; it has to actually be a role the model can execute reliably. Together these two findings triangulate a capability threshold: below some floor of model reliability, rigid structure constrains errors that self-organization would otherwise amplify or fail to catch; above that floor, the same rigidity becomes the thing costing accuracy, tokens, and time that LATTE and DyLAN measure.

## Is there a clear best pattern?

Yes, and it is more specific than "dynamic beats static" — it's a particular *hybrid* shape that two independent, large-scale studies converge on despite testing different tasks, models, and architectures. The 25,000-run, 8-model study tested four points on the centralized-to-autonomous spectrum and found that **Sequential** — agent *order* fixed, but each agent autonomously chooses its own role/contribution within that slot — beat both **Coordinator** (fully centralized, static role assignment, the closest analogue to MetaGPT) by 14% and **Shared** (fully autonomous, no fixed order at all) by 44%. The authors name this the "endogeneity paradox": the best-performing design is neither maximal top-down control nor maximal unstructured autonomy, but minimal imposed structure plus roles that emerge endogenously within it. LATTE lands on the identical shape from a different direction — its Lead agent holds centralized global visibility while Workers propose and claim work locally, a deliberate split of authority rather than a pure hierarchy or a pure peer network — and that hybrid beat every pure alternative it was tested against (MetaGPT, static Leader-Worker, full decentralization) on accuracy *and* cost *and* time simultaneously, using roughly a fifth of MetaGPT's tokens for more than double the accuracy. Two research groups, working independently on different problems, both find that a little fixed scaffolding plus autonomous content beats either extreme — that convergence is the strongest single claim this investigation can make.

Three qualifications keep it from being a universal answer rather than a strong default. **It inherits the capability threshold** from the section above: the same 25k-run study that establishes the hybrid's advantage also shows it inverting for weaker models, where free-form role emergence *loses* to rigid, externally-imposed structure. **The task has to be worth the coordination overhead** — LATTE's authors flag that its planning overhead can exceed the benefit on short, simple tasks a single agent would handle fine, which is the same threshold question the [swarm-tax synthesis](swarm-tax-multi-agent-cost-benefit.md) raises before any pattern-selection question is relevant at all. **Structure isn't always the dominant lever even within a winning architecture** — Meta-Team's ablation found that letting a team restructure itself (the piece most comparable to dynamic role reallocation) contributed the smallest of three gains, 2.0 of a 10.6-point total, with individual agents reflecting on and improving their own behavior contributing more; and TeamBench's verifier finding is a reminder that a role only helps if the model can execute it reliably; a hybrid coordination *shape* does not fix an individually unreliable *role*.

## Verdict

**Reject** "static roles are among the more successful patterns" as stated. **Confirm** the adjacent, narrower claim the hypothesis was probably gesturing at: explicit, non-redundant role differentiation reliably beats no differentiation or homogeneous agents, and this holds whether the differentiation is fixed or adaptive. Where the literature isolates fixedness itself as the variable, the better-controlled and more recent evidence (LATTE, DyLAN, the 25k-run study, AgentNet) consistently favors *dynamic* allocation at current frontier-model capability, with static/rigid structure only reasserting an advantage below a model-capability threshold this source set does not precisely locate. Practically, this converges with [Deep Agents' interpreter-authored dynamic subagents](../concepts/deepagents-interpreters.md) and its [async/dynamic delegation](../concepts/deepagents-delegation.md) being architected the way they are rather than as MetaGPT-style fixed pipelines, and it sharpens [multi-agent-frameworks.md](../concepts/multi-agent-frameworks.md)'s framing of MetaGPT/ChatDev's fixed-role assembly-line pattern as one point in the design space rather than a default to reach for.
