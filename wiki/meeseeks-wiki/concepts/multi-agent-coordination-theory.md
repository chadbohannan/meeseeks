# Multi-Agent Coordination Theory

Whether a group of agents actually coordinates — as opposed to merely being deployed near each other — is a question with a theoretical answer from classical multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) and an empirical answer from recent LLM-agent benchmarks, and the two answers do not always agree. This page traces coordination from its game-theoretic and RL foundations through to concrete, measured mechanisms in LLM-based systems, and is the companion to [multi-agent-collaboration-taxonomy.md](multi-agent-collaboration-taxonomy.md), which classifies the *shape* of a multi-agent system rather than whether that shape actually works.

## The classical bedrock: MARL, CTDE, and non-stationarity

The pre-LLM theoretical foundation comes from multi-agent reinforcement learning, surveyed comprehensively by Huh and Mohapatra (2023/2024, arxiv.org/abs/2312.10256). MARL sits at the intersection of three lineages: game theory supplies the formal apparatus for modeling agent interactions, payoff structures, and strategic equilibria (Nash equilibria, zero-sum vs. general-sum games); machine learning supplies the optimization machinery (function approximation, gradient-based policy improvement); and MARL combines these so that multiple agents learn concurrently in a shared environment where each agent's optimal policy depends on the — constantly changing — policies of the others.

Three concepts from this survey recur throughout the rest of this page. **Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)** lets agents access shared information during training but requires them to act on local information alone at deployment — a middle ground between fully centralized and fully decentralized learning. **Non-stationarity** is the core theoretical obstacle: because every agent's policy is changing simultaneously, each individual agent faces a constantly shifting learning target, which complicates convergence guarantees. **Credit assignment** is the problem of attributing how much each individual agent contributed to a shared or global reward signal, addressed via counterfactual reasoning and reward-shaping techniques. The survey lists scalability ("scaling learning to hundreds or thousands of agents remains computationally prohibitive"), communication overhead, and the general absence of convergence/optimality proofs for decentralized algorithms as open challenges — a sober baseline against which the LLM-swarm literature below should be read.

This classical framing matters directly for LLM agents. The LLM-Coordination benchmark (Anonymous authors, 2023, arxiv.org/abs/2310.03903) reports that "LLM agents, unlike RL methods, exhibit robustness to unseen partners" in zero-shot coordination settings — a direct, empirically observed counterpoint to the partner-generalization brittleness that self-play-trained RL policies are notorious for. Read against the MARL survey, this is best understood as LLM agents sidestepping the non-stationarity problem rather than solving it: an LLM's policy isn't trained against a fixed population of co-players the way an RL policy is, so it never specializes into the kind of narrow, partner-specific convention that breaks when a new partner appears.

## Kernel drift: a physics-style account of why coordination sometimes fails

A 2025/2026 paper (arxiv.org/abs/2511.23315) gives the most mechanistically precise account in this source set of *why* decentralized coordination emerges, fluctuates, or collapses. Using fully Independent Q-Learning (IQL) — agents that learn purely from local reward with no shared training infrastructure, no communication, and no awareness that other learning agents exist — as a deliberately minimal testbed, the paper formalizes MARL non-stationarity as **kernel drift**: from any one agent's point of view, the effective transition kernel of its environment is not stationary, because every other agent's simultaneous policy update silently reshapes what "the environment" looks like. This drift competes against synchronization forces that pull agents toward mutual coordination.

Sweeping environment size (L) and agent density (ρ), the authors map three regimes: a **coordinated and stable phase** where synchronization dominates kernel drift, a **fragile transition region** where the two forces compete and outcomes become volatile and sensitive to small perturbations, and a **jammed/disordered phase** where kernel drift dominates and cooperation collapses into deadlock. A sharp "double Instability Ridge" in (L, ρ) space separates these regimes, echoing genuine physical phase transitions. Strikingly, removing agent identifiers eliminates kernel drift entirely, and the whole three-phase structure collapses with it — evidence that it is *inter-agent asymmetry* (agents being individually distinguishable), not multi-agency per se, that drives the instability. Temporal alignment between agents' actions also turns out to be necessary for sustained cooperation; desynchronized timing pushes systems toward the fragile or jammed regimes even at otherwise favorable density and scale.

Although framed in classical tabular-RL terms, this phase-structure picture is directly portable to the LLM-swarm findings below: SwarmBench's finding that LLM swarms struggle with adaptive strategy formation under uncertainty, and CodeCRDT's finding that parallel-coordination benefits collapse above roughly 20 agents, are both consistent with coordination being something that holds only within a bounded regime of scale, density, and synchronization — outside of which systems drift into fragile or jammed states. The paper leaves open what plays the role of "kernel drift" when the thing being updated each round is not a value function but a stream of natural-language reasoning conditioned on other agents' outputs.

## Emergent coordination, measured

A companion 2025 paper by Christoph Riedl (arxiv.org/abs/2510.05174) asks what happens when no explicit coordination mechanism — no shared protocol, no designated leader, no communication schema — is imposed on a group of LLM agents. It documents three emergent phenomena: **spontaneous protocol development**, where agents invent their own signaling conventions on the fly to solve tasks more efficiently; **unintended alignment**, where independently operating agents' behaviors converge over the course of interaction, implying mutual adaptation without being told to adapt; and **emergent specialization**, where individual agents within a team spontaneously gravitate toward distinct functional roles that were never assigned by the experimental design.

Methodologically, Riedl measures this using information-theoretic concepts of *synergy* and *integrated information* — quantifying how much of a team's behavior is attributable to genuine multi-agent interaction versus simple aggregation of independent individual behaviors. The paper's central argument is that this coordination is a genuine collective-level phenomenon, not a scaled-up aggregation of individual behavior, which means team-level behavior is not reliably predictable from the individually-specified behavior of each component agent. This is worth reading alongside AgentNet's claim (below) that decentralized specialization can *outperform* centralized designs: Riedl's finding suggests such specialization is not something the system architect necessarily designed at all, but something that can emerge unbidden from interaction dynamics — for better or worse.

## Concrete coordination mechanisms without central control

Two 2025 systems papers show what decentralized coordination looks like when built deliberately, rather than emerging by accident.

**AgentNet** (arxiv.org/abs/2504.00587) replaces the still-dominant central-orchestrator pattern (seen in MetaGPT- and ChatDev-style systems) with a fully decentralized architecture: agents self-organize into a dynamically evolving directed acyclic graph (DAG), routing tasks toward whichever peer's demonstrated expertise best matches the task at hand, with no central coordinator making that assignment. A retrieval-augmented (RAG) memory layer lets each agent's local expertise compound over time. The authors argue this sidesteps the orchestrator's structural weaknesses — throughput bottleneck, static roles, single point of failure, and forced information exposure in cross-organizational settings. On a full-text follow-up read of the paper (the raw capture only had abstract-level detail), the quantitative results are: on MATH, AgentNet scores 92.86% vs. 89.28% for the strongest single-agent baseline (Synapse), a +3.58-point gain; on Big-Bench Hard, 94.00% vs. 92.00% (+2.00); on API-Bank function-calling, 30.00% vs. 28.00% for the best baseline (AFLOW). Against centralized multi-agent baselines, the paper reports MetaGPT reaching only 53.00% on Logical QA under a GPT-4o-mini backbone, which the authors cite as evidence of the centralized-orchestrator scalability ceiling that motivates AgentNet's design. These numbers should be read as one paper's own reported results rather than independently replicated; no external benchmark comparison across papers was available in this source set. A later, more tightly controlled comparison corroborates the direction of this result: the [static-vs-dynamic-roles synthesis](../syntheses/static-vs-dynamic-roles-multi-agent-success.md) covers LATTE, which benchmarks its own dynamic coordination graph against MetaGPT under matched team size and models and finds an even larger gap (79.7% vs. 33.9% task accuracy), isolating "can the coordination structure adapt after planning" as the specific variable responsible for roughly half of that gap.

**CodeCRDT** (arxiv.org/html/2510.18893) is the most directly Meeseeks-relevant source in this set: it studies parallel LLM coding agents that coordinate by observing a shared Conflict-Free Replicated Data Type (CRDT) rather than exchanging messages — a stigmergic pattern in which agents detect what work remains by watching changes to shared state, then act. Task assignment uses an optimistic-write protocol: an agent writes its own ID into a TODO's `assignedTo` field, waits 50ms for CRDT synchronization, and proceeds only if it observes its own ID as the winning value — a protocol the authors prove gives at most one agent per TODO after convergence, because the underlying CRDT (Yjs) resolves concurrent writes deterministically via lexicographic (logical clock, clientID) ordering. This is the same "coordinate via shared, observable state" strategy that the LLM-Coordination benchmark's Theory-of-Mind findings (below) suggest is attractive precisely because it routes around LLMs' documented weakness at modeling other agents' mental states directly.

CodeCRDT's results are a useful corrective against reading decentralized coordination as uniformly beneficial. Across 600 trials (six TypeScript/React tasks, 50 runs each in sequential vs. parallel mode, Claude Sonnet 4.5), raw wall-clock performance was highly task-dependent: a 21.1% *speedup* on Tic-Tac-Toe versus a 39.4% *slowdown* on Algorithm Visualizer, averaging a 13.1% overall slowdown for parallel versus sequential. Much of this gap traces to parallel agents producing 82–189% more code (redundant safety/optimization additions), and when time is normalized per character generated, parallel wins for 5 of 6 tasks by 11–52%. Code quality effects were mixed in direction, not just magnitude: parallel coordination improved the performance dimension strongly (+25%, Cohen's d = 1.51) but *hurt* general code quality (-7.7%, d = -0.71) and accessibility (-5.6%, d = -0.59). The CRDT substrate itself delivered perfect character-level convergence (zero merge conflicts across all 600 trials), but manual inspection of a 10% sample found 5–10% *semantic* conflicts — duplicate declarations, type mismatches, broken references — that CRDTs structurally cannot detect because they operate below the semantic level. Scalability analysis identified observation-processing overhead (O(N×updates), since every state update triggers callbacks in all N agents) as the dominant bottleneck around N≈25–30 agents, with a projected optimum of 3–5 agents for low-coupling tasks (peak ~2.05× speedup near N=3) degrading to break-even around N≈20. The authors' central finding — that component interdependency ("coupling") of the task, not agent count alone, is the primary determinant of whether parallel coordination helps or hurts — is a direct empirical instance of the kernel-drift paper's claim that coordination holds only within a bounded regime rather than scaling indefinitely.

## What LLM agents actually do under swarm-like constraints

Two benchmark papers measure LLM coordination ability directly rather than architecting a substrate for it.

The LLM-Coordination benchmark (arxiv.org/abs/2310.03903) separates *behavioral* coordination performance from the underlying *cognitive* sub-skills it depends on, via two tracks: Agentic Coordination, where LLMs act as live participants in four pure-coordination games, and CoordQA, a static 198-question test isolating Environment Comprehension, Theory of Mind reasoning, and Joint Planning. The key finding is a split: LLMs perform relatively well when the correct action can largely be inferred from the environment's state alone, but perform substantially worse when a task requires actively modeling a partner's mental state and intentions — the core Theory-of-Mind demand. This split matters for interpreting AgentNet and CodeCRDT: both largely sidestep the Theory-of-Mind problem by making coordination state observable in shared structure (a DAG, a CRDT) rather than requiring agents to model each other's minds — which, per this benchmark's findings, is exactly the sub-skill where LLMs are weakest. Coordinating through shared observable state may be popular in practice precisely because it routes around a documented capability gap rather than closing it.

SwarmBench (arxiv.org/abs/2505.04364) pushes further, stripping away global state and central control entirely: agents operate in a configurable 2D grid, receiving only local sensory input through a k×k view window and communicating only with nearby neighbors — informational constraints that define genuine swarm systems, as distinct from systems with a shared global blackboard or central planner. Across five classic swarm-robotics tasks (pursuit, synchronization, foraging, flocking, transport), evaluated zero-shot against models including DeepSeek-v3 and o4-mini, performance varies by task and shows some minimal emergent coordination in places, but the dominant, explicitly cautionary finding is that current LLMs "significantly struggle with robust long-range planning and adaptive strategy formation under the uncertainty inherent in these decentralized scenarios." This is a necessary counterweight to the more architecture-optimistic results from AgentNet and CodeCRDT: it suggests that even when a well-designed decentralized coordination substrate exists, the LLM agents' own reasoning — not the substrate — may still be the bottleneck for swarm-style tasks that require long-range planning under genuine partial observability.

## Synthesis: theory, substrate, and reasoning are three separate bottlenecks

Reading these seven sources together, coordination failure or success in an LLM-agent swarm has at least three largely independent causes, and the sources in this set each speak to a different one. The **theoretical/dynamical** cause is what the MARL survey and the kernel-drift paper describe: non-stationarity and the drift/synchronization balance bound what scale and density of coordination is even achievable, regardless of agent intelligence. The **substrate/architecture** cause is what AgentNet and CodeCRDT address: whether the coordination mechanism itself (DAG routing, CRDT observation) avoids centralized bottlenecks and gives agents a reliable, low-conflict way to divide labor — but CodeCRDT's coupling-dependent results show a good substrate is necessary, not sufficient, for a net speedup. The **reasoning/capability** cause is what LLM-Coordination and SwarmBench measure directly: LLM agents' own Theory-of-Mind and long-range planning limits under partial observability, which no amount of substrate engineering resolves because the substrate only decides how information reaches an agent, not whether the agent can reason well from it. Riedl's emergent-coordination paper sits across all three, since the collective-level behavior it measures with synergy and integrated-information metrics is the joint product of dynamics, substrate, and per-agent reasoning at once — which is also why its central safety claim, that team behavior isn't predictable from individually-specified agent behavior, follows naturally from having three semi-independent bottlenecks rather than one. Locating a given multi-agent system on the [collaboration taxonomy](multi-agent-collaboration-taxonomy.md)'s structure/strategy axes tells you which of these bottlenecks it is most exposed to: centralized structures inherit the orchestrator throughput ceiling AgentNet argues against, while decentralized/model-based strategies inherit the Theory-of-Mind gap LLM-Coordination measures.
