# Multi-Agent Collaboration Taxonomy

When more than one LLM-driven agent works on the same problem, the design space for how they relate to each other is large enough that two independent 2024–2025 surveys converge on similar vocabulary for describing it. The broader survey on LLM-based multi-agent systems (Guo et al., 2024/2025) defines an LLM-MAS as "a system that includes a collection of generative agents capable of interacting and collaborating within a shared environmental setting," where the LLM is the core control/reasoning mechanism for each agent. The companion survey on collaboration mechanisms (2025) supplies a five-dimension taxonomy — actors, types, structures, strategies, coordination protocols — for classifying any such system regardless of domain. Together these give a shared vocabulary for describing where a swarm of coding or reasoning agents sits in the design space, which matters for reading [multi-agent-coordination-theory.md](multi-agent-coordination-theory.md): the taxonomy here describes the shape of a system, while that page describes whether and how coordination actually emerges within a given shape.

## What defines a generative agent

The Guo et al. survey characterizes each agent in an LLM-MAS by four essential capabilities. **Profiling** establishes persona and specialization through role descriptions given in natural language or via customized prompts. **Memory** is multi-layered (long-term, short-term, sensory) and enables coherent behavior across long trajectories. **Planning** formulates behavior patterns and sub-goals across extended timeframes. **Action** executes interactions with the environment — tool calls, messages, and similar effects. Agents communicate either through natural language, which is highly interpretable but harder to optimize algorithmically, or through custom/structured content, which is more optimizable but opaque to human observers.

The environment surrounding these agents decomposes into three structural elements: **tools** that translate an agent's instructions into concrete outcomes via a defined action space, **rules** that define communication patterns and behavioral constraints among agents, and **intervention** — the external interface through which humans or supervisory systems can step in. This intervention layer is the conceptual analog of Meeseeks' own dismiss-without-kill model, where a human can step away from an agent's console without halting the agent itself.

## The five-dimensional taxonomy of collaboration mechanisms

The collaboration-mechanisms survey (arxiv.org/pdf/2501.06322) decomposes any multi-agent architecture along five axes, letting researchers compare systems as different as a debate-based reasoning pipeline, a simulated society, and a robotic swarm on common ground.

**Actors** are the agents themselves — their model architecture, memory, objectives, environment, inputs, and outputs — and systems vary in how many actors participate and how specialized each is.

**Types** describe the nature of the relationship between agents. *Cooperation* (shared goals) is exemplified by CAMEL, AutoGen, MetaGPT, and AgentVerse. *Competition* (opposed goals) appears in LLMArena and the critic phase of LEGO-style debate frameworks. *Coopetition* — simultaneously cooperating on some dimensions while competing on others — shows up in Mixture-of-Experts routing and negotiation scenarios.

**Structures** describe the topology of the agent group. *Centralized* structures route through a coordinator, as in LLM-Blender, AgentCoord, and federated-learning-style approaches. *Decentralized/distributed* structures spread coordination across peers, as in AgentCF, MetaGPT's role network, ChatDev, ProAgent, and Park et al.'s generative-agents town. *Hierarchical* structures layer the two, as in DyLAN, CAMEL, AgentVerse, and ChatDev. Note that several named systems (MetaGPT, ChatDev, CAMEL, AgentVerse) recur across more than one structural bucket in the survey's own classification, which suggests real systems often blend structures rather than sitting cleanly in one cell — a point worth remembering when locating any given swarm architecture on this taxonomy.

**Strategies** describe how an agent decides to act relative to others. *Rule-based* strategies impose predefined rules with high predictability — majority voting, debate protocols, consensus-seeking. *Role-based* strategies assign distinct predefined roles and divide specialized work, as in MetaGPT's standard operating procedures (SOPs) and AgentVerse's recruitment/evaluation roles. *Model-based* strategies use probabilistic decision-making grounded in Theory of Mind — an agent reasoning about what another agent knows or intends. This taxonomy treats "role-based" as one strategy among three without distinguishing whether roles are assigned once or reassigned at runtime — a distinction that turns out to matter more than the taxonomy's own categories suggest. The [static-vs-dynamic-roles synthesis](../syntheses/static-vs-dynamic-roles-multi-agent-success.md) tests that specific variable directly and finds that *role differentiation* (this strategy axis) reliably helps, while *permanence* of the assignment does not — and where cleanly isolated, adaptive reassignment tends to outperform a fixed assignment of the same roles.

**Coordination protocols** are the concrete synchronization mechanisms: debate/discussion (turn-based competitive argument exchange), majority voting, peer review (iterative critique-and-refine cycles), consensus-seeking, ranking/selection (pairwise ranking as in LLM-Blender, or dynamic agent selection as in DyLAN), and the assembly-line model of sequential role-based processing used by MetaGPT. As the survey puts it, coordination "extends beyond the functionality of individual collaboration channels, focusing instead on the relationships and interactions among multiple channels" — the protocol layer is about how channels compose, not just what any one channel does.

## A terminology caveat: "swarm" as a biasing label

"Swarm" gets applied to nearly every multi-agent LLM system in casual and marketing usage, but it names one specific cell of the taxonomy above — decentralized structure, local-only information, no central coordinator — and applying it more broadly smuggles in expectations the underlying architecture doesn't earn. [SwarmBench](multi-agent-coordination-theory.md#what-llm-agents-actually-do-under-swarm-like-constraints), a benchmark built specifically to measure genuine swarm behavior, has to explicitly define "true swarm constraints" (a local k×k view, communication restricted to nearby neighbors) precisely to separate real swarms from "systems with a shared global blackboard or central planner" — an implicit admission that most systems wearing the label don't meet it. By that definition, the dominant, best-evidenced architecture in this whole source set — a central orchestrator dispatching isolated workers that report back to it, as [Claude Code's subagents](../systems/claude-code.md#capability-surface), [Deep Agents' delegation](deepagents-delegation.md), and [Anthropic's own research system](../syntheses/swarm-tax-multi-agent-cost-benefit.md) all do — is not a swarm at all under a strict reading, yet is routinely described as one.

The mislabeling isn't just semantic pedantry; it imports a specific, often false expectation of *robustness through redundancy* — the biological intuition that many independently-noisy agents average out each other's errors, the way ants or bees do. LLM agent errors are correlated rather than independent (shared training, shared prompts, shared blind spots), which is exactly why the multi-agent voting/ensemble systems in the [swarm-tax synthesis](../syntheses/swarm-tax-multi-agent-cost-benefit.md) can show DeepMind-measured error *amplification* — a reported factor of 17.2x — instead of the error-cancellation a biological swarm framing would predict. [OpenAI's own framework](multi-agent-frameworks.md#peer-to-peer-dynamic-handoff-openai-swarm-and-camel) is a case in point: named "Swarm," its actual mechanism is centralized, client-side, handoff-based orchestration with no decentralization requirement and no local-information constraint — the name rides a connotation the architecture doesn't structurally provide. Even sources critical of multi-agent hype aren't immune: the "swarm tax" framing this wiki also uses is a useful piece of vocabulary, but it too uses "swarm" as a generic stand-in for "any multi-agent system," continuing the same conflation even while arguing against the systems it describes.

The more precise vocabulary is already on this page: **structure** (centralized / decentralized / hierarchical) and **strategy** (rule-based / role-based / model-based) from the five-dimensional taxonomy above say what an architecture actually does; "swarm" should be reserved for the decentralized, locally-informed corner of that space — CAMEL's peer-to-peer role-play, AgentNet's DAG routing, CodeCRDT's stigmergic coordination — rather than used as a synonym for "more than one agent."

## Applications: solving, simulating, evaluating

The Guo et al. survey organizes the broader field's use cases into three buckets. **Solving complex tasks** splits into reasoning frameworks — multi-stage pipelines like ChatDev for software development, collective decision-making via voting/debate (GEDI, Multi-Agents-Debate), and self-refine frameworks where agents iteratively critique their own or each other's outputs (ReConcile, LLM-discussion) — and communication optimization, covering both speed optimization (compressed non-verbal channels between agents) and distributed discussion for problems where no single agent has complete information (iAgents). Representative systems here include ChatDev, MetaGPT, AgentScope (message-exchange with distributed deployment), and OpenAI's Swarm (lightweight orchestration).

**Simulating specific scenarios** spans social-domain work — Park et al.'s 25-agent generative-agents town, social-media simulation (YuLan-Rec), emotional propagation (S3), social-movement modeling at roughly 10^6-agent scale (SoMoSiMu), game scenarios (Werewolf, Avalon), city-scale modeling (Urban Generative Intelligence), and professional-domain simulations like Agent Hospital and AgentCourt — plus physical-domain work in mobility and wireless-network coordination.

**Evaluating generative agents** covers arena-based assessment of long-horizon planning, leadership, and competition (Auction Arena, LLMArena), theory-of-mind and emotional-understanding benchmarks (MuMA-ToM), and training uses such as supervised fine-tuning on multi-agent-generated data (CoEvol), multi-agent RL to overcome single-agent training biases (demonstrated in Werewolf gameplay), and data synthesis via multi-agent interaction (Stable Alignment).

## Known open challenges

Both surveys converge on a similar set of unresolved problems, which is notable given they were written independently. **Hallucination and its propagation** is flagged by both: the Guo et al. survey notes that agents exhibit probabilistic hallucination during interaction, with mitigation techniques reducing but not eliminating it, while the collaboration-mechanisms survey calls the multi-round version of this "cascading hallucinations" — error propagation compounding across sustained multi-agent interaction. This is the same phenomenon Guo et al. separately name the **accumulative effect**: errors compound round over round, undermining reliability, with attempted fixes including rule-based error-correcting intermediaries and scalability-oriented architectures like Internet-of-Agents.

**Efficiency** is the other recurring theme. Guo et al. describe an "efficiency explosion": autoregressive LLM inference is inherently slow, each agent action may require multiple LLM queries for retrieval, planning, and execution, and cost/latency scales poorly — potentially exponentially — as agent count grows. Partial mitigations include replacing peripheral agents with rule-based logic, though this breaks down once an agent needs a genuinely complex action space. The collaboration-mechanisms survey names the same concern as "scalability": managing communication overhead and complexity as systems grow large.

**Lack of objective group-behavior metrics** is the shared evaluation gap. Guo et al. note that multi-agent environments are diverse and unpredictable enough that current evaluation largely reduces to comparing distributions between simulated and real environments, giving limited insight into internal LLM-MAS processes; they also flag the absence of unified benchmarks that let systems be compared within the same category, or a common framework spanning individual-agent and population-level assessment. The collaboration-mechanisms survey echoes this as a need for "comprehensive evaluation" and standardized benchmarking across diverse MAS designs, and additionally raises ethical risk/safety governance, conflict resolution (ensuring competition stays constructive rather than degenerating), and robustness to individual agent failure or underperformance as open problems on the road toward what it calls "artificial collective intelligence" — the long-run goal a mature taxonomy of collaboration mechanisms is meant to serve.

Two further challenges specific to generative agents round out the picture: **generalized alignment**, where agents struggle to authentically depict diverse personality traits because foundation models are trained toward a narrow, aligned behavioral distribution; and **limited long-text capability**, where agents lose track of earlier information in complex, extended interactions due to context-window constraints, degrading performance on knowledge-intensive tasks (the survey points to multi-step reasoning approaches, citing OpenAI's o1, as a potential mitigation).

Where this taxonomy describes system *shape*, the question of whether a given shape actually produces working coordination — and under what conditions it collapses — is the subject of [multi-agent-coordination-theory.md](multi-agent-coordination-theory.md), which grounds the same named systems (MetaGPT, decentralized DAG architectures like AgentNet, observation-driven substrates like CodeCRDT) in classical MARL theory and direct empirical measurement.
